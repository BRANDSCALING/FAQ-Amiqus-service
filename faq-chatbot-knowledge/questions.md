**SECTION 1: THE LEGAL FRAMEWORK**

**Q1. What is 'exempt accommodation' — and is it a loophole?**

Exempt accommodation is defined in paragraph 4(10) of Schedule 3 to the Housing Benefit and Council Tax Benefit (Consequential Provisions) Regulations 2006. It has been part of UK law since 1996 and was deliberately designed to ensure that vulnerable people receive adequate housing with appropriate support. It is not a loophole — it is a fully legislated framework.

**Q2. What legislation governs exempt accommodation?**

Three key pieces of legislation underpin the framework:

- The Housing Benefit Regulations 2006 (Schedule 3, Paragraph 4(10))
- The Supported Housing (Regulatory Oversight) Act 2023
- The Social Housing (Regulation) Act 2023

The Regulator of Social Housing oversees all Registered Providers operating within this framework.

**Q3. Could the government ever scrap exempt accommodation?**

No government has proposed abolishing exempt accommodation since its introduction in 1996. It has survived multiple changes of government — Labour, Coalition, Conservative, and Labour again. The government is actively spending tens of millions of pounds regulating and expanding the sector. Its own research shows that every £1 spent on supported housing saves money in reduced demand on NHS, criminal justice, and homelessness services.

**Q4. What is the Supported Housing (Regulatory Oversight) Act 2023?**

This Act introduces mandatory licensing for supported housing providers, national standards for accommodation quality and support, and fit and proper person tests for operators. It is being implemented by approximately March 2027. It is designed to remove rogue operators and strengthen the position of legitimate, compliant providers — which is exactly what the UCWS model is built around.

**Q5. Does the Renters Rights Bill affect this strategy?**

The Renters Rights Bill applies to Assured Shorthold Tenancies (ASTs). The UCWS model primarily uses an Excluded Licence Agreement, which is not an AST. Therefore, the Renters Rights Bill does not directly affect the core strategy.

**SECTION 2: WHY RENTS ARE HIGHER THAN LHA — AND WHY THERE IS NO BENEFIT CAP**

**Q6. How can rents be £280–£350 per room per week when LHA rates are so much lower?**

Exempt accommodation rents are not capped by Local Housing Allowance (LHA) rates. The rent includes the cost of providing mandatory support and supervision to vulnerable people. The government pays above-market rates because the alternatives are far more expensive — hospital beds cost £2,000–£3,500 per week, prison costs £1,100 per week, and temporary accommodation costs approximately £1,800+ per month. Supported housing under the exempt framework is, by a significant margin, the most cost-effective option.

**Q7. Why can I not deal with DWP or the RP myself?**

To deal with DWP you need to be a Registered Provider or an Official Partner. Our vetted RP will only make payment to Allianz Housing and does not have the capacity to deal directly with hundreds of individual landlords.

**Q8. Is there a benefit cap on exempt accommodation?**

No. Exempt accommodation is excluded from the overall benefit cap. This is a deliberate policy decision because the tenants housed under this framework have support needs that cannot be met within standard benefit limits.

**SECTION 3: WHAT IS AN RSL AND HOW DOES THE STRUCTURE WORK?**

**Q9. What is an RSL?**

RSL stands for Registered Social Landlord, also known as a Registered Provider (RP). In the UCWS model, the RP becomes the legal landlord by leasing the property from you. They are registered with the Regulator of Social Housing, which allows the Local Authority to claim 100% subsidy from DWP for the Housing Benefit. Without an RP as the landlord, the LA only receives a partial subsidy. In the UCWS model, the RP receives payment direct from DWP.

**Q10. Can I bring my own RSL?**

No. UCWS only works with a panel of pre-vetted Registered Providers. You cannot bring your own RSL. This is to ensure quality, compliance, and consistency across the programme. You can request your RSL to apply to us at info@brandscaling.co.uk where we can then vet them.

**Q11. How exactly does the payment flow work?**

DWP pays Housing Benefit directly to the RP (normally it only funds the Local Authority, who then pay the RP). The RP pays Allianz Housing the next day. Allianz Housing then pays the landlord on the next weekly payment run, which occurs every Thursday. The first payment takes approximately 6 weeks after the tenant moves in, due to Housing Benefit processing time.

**Q12. What does the 25% fee cover?**

The 25% is split as follows:

- 13% to the RP — legal landlord responsibilities, regulatory compliance
- 6% to Allianz Housing — operational management, payment processing
- 1% to the software platform — onboarding properties compliantly
- 5% to Tenant Services — tenant management and support services

The remaining 75% is your gross income, from which you cover your property costs (mortgage, bills, insurance, etc.).

**SECTION 4: WHY THIS IS NOT JUST HOUSING — IT MUST INCLUDE SUPPORT**

**Q13. Do the tenants need support?**

Yes. Tenants must be vulnerable and in need of care, support, or supervision. The property only qualifies as exempt accommodation because support is being provided. Without the mandatory support element, the higher rents cannot be justified and the framework does not apply.

**Q14. What types of vulnerable people are eligible?**

Eligible categories include people with mental health problems, physical disabilities, learning disabilities, people experiencing homelessness, homeless families, people with drug or alcohol problems, prison leavers, survivors of domestic abuse, vulnerable young people and care leavers, refugees, veterans, and older people with support needs.

**Q15. What kind of support is provided — and is CQC registration required?**

The support provided is housing-related support, which includes budgeting and financial management, tenancy sustainment, employment and education coaching, community integration, life skills development, and exit planning. This is not personal care (such as washing, dressing, or medication management), and therefore CQC registration is not required.

**Q16. Who provides the tenant support?**

Companies approved by the RP provide tenant sustainment services at a cost per week per occupied unit. This covers things like suitability assessments, intake, coaching, employment support, community integration, and exit planning. This cost is included within the 25% deductions.

**SECTION 5: PLANNING, LICENSING, AND ARTICLE 4**

**Q17. What is C3(b) and why does it matter for landlords using the exempt accommodation framework?**

C3(b) applies to a property where up to six people live together as a single household and receive care. If a property genuinely operates this way under the exempt accommodation framework, it may remain within C3 planning use and not require a change of use. This depends on the strategy and how the property is structured and managed in practice. We cover this in detail during training, including how to structure properties correctly.

**Q18. Do I need an HMO licence?**

Up to 6 residents receiving support fall under the C3(b) use class and do not require an HMO licence. The property operates under an Excluded Licence Agreement, not an HMO licence.

**Q19. My property is in an Article 4 area — does that matter?**

Article 4 directions typically remove permitted development rights for changes from C3 (dwellinghouse) to C4 (small HMO). If a property genuinely operates within C3(b) — up to six residents living together as a single household and receiving care — it may remain within the C3 use class and not trigger a C3 to C4 change. However, whether Article 4 applies depends on how the property is structured and how the Local Authority classifies its actual use. It is not automatic.

We cover this in detail during training, including how to structure the property correctly.

**SECTION 6: RISKS, VOIDS, AND HOW TO MITIGATE THEM**

**Q20. What are the key risks?**

The key risks include void periods (the landlord is responsible), property damage (covered by insurance), tenant issues (managed by the tenant support services company), and lender restrictions (some lenders may need to approve the arrangement). The training covers risk mitigation strategies for each of these in detail.

**Q21. Who is responsible for void periods?**

The landlord is responsible for all void periods. There is no guaranteed rent under this model. However, the demand for supported housing significantly outstrips supply — the government estimates an additional 361,700 to 640,700 units are needed by 2040 — which helps to minimise void risk in practice.

**Q22. What happens if the tenant gets a job?**

Housing Benefit entitlement is based on the tenant's individual circumstances and income. If a tenant begins working, their benefits may be reassessed. In many cases, tenants in exempt accommodation continue to receive Housing Benefit, but the amount can change depending on earnings and overall benefit status. If a tenant's income increases significantly, their Housing Benefit may reduce in line with standard benefit rules. We explain how benefit assessments work in more detail during training so landlords understand how income changes can affect payments.

**Q23. What is the eviction process under an excluded licence?**

Under an Excluded Licence Agreement, the notice period is typically 7 days. This is significantly faster than an AST under the new Renters Rights Act. The Excluded Licence does not give the tenant exclusive possession, and the provider has the right to enter as needed. The exact process is covered in the training.

**SECTION 7: PRACTICAL QUESTIONS**

**Q24. Do I need a special mortgage?**

No specific mortgage type is mandated, but you will need lender permission if the property is mortgaged. The programme can be used with properties held in a personal name or a limited company. We cannot give mortgage advice. One of the training modules is delivered by a specialist mortgage broker.

**Q25. Who pays the bills?**

In most cases under the HMO strategy (Strategies 1 and 2), the landlord pays all bills. In the single-let strategy (Strategy 3, for studios and 1-bed properties), the tenant pays them. We cover this in more depth in the training modules.

**Q26. Who is responsible for council tax?**

Council tax is the landlord's responsibility in the void period. It is the tenant's responsibility on all self-contained properties. For HMOs, it is usually the landlord's responsibility unless the property qualifies for an exemption or specific arrangements are in place. The training covers this in detail.

**Q27. What compliance certificates are required?**

Some of the certificates required include the Gas Safety Certificate, EICR (Electrical Installation Condition Report), EPC, Fire Risk Assessment, Emergency Lighting Certificate, Fire Detection Certificate, PAT Testing, Legionella Risk Assessment, Asbestos Report, Damp Report, Building Insurance, Title Deed, and Letter of Authority. The full checklist and approved contractors are provided during training.

**Q28. How long does it take to get a property earning?**

Typically 3–4 weeks to get the property compliant and find a tenant. After the tenant moves in, approximately 6 weeks for DWP to process the first Housing Benefit payment in arrears. After that, payments are received every 4 weeks and paid to you by Allianz Housing on the Thursday payment run.

**Q29. Does the programme work across the UK?**

The programme currently operates in England only. Wales expansion is planned but no confirmed date yet. Scotland and Northern Ireland are not yet on the roadmap.

**Q30. Can international investors participate?**

Yes. The programme is open to international investors. You do not need to be a UK national or resident. Training is delivered online and the system can be managed remotely.

**SECTION 19: USING THE DEAL ANALYSER**

**Q87. What is the Deal Analyser and where do I find it?**

The Deal Analyser is the tool that tells you whether a property deal stacks up under the UCWS exempt-accommodation model — it gives you a clear GO or NO-GO based on your numbers. Open it from the portal (the "Deal Analyser" link). The screen is titled "Deal Analyser — UCWS Exempt Accommodation". It is for illustration only and is not financial advice.

**Q88. Which strategy should I choose — UC HMO, UC Joint, or UC Self-Contained?**

There are three tabs at the top; pick the one that matches your property. UC HMO (Strategy 1) is a 3-bed-plus house with two reception rooms turned into a 4-bed shared house, charged per room per week. UC Joint (Strategy 2) is a 2-bed flat or house run as a 2-bed shared, also per room per week. UC Self-Contained (Strategy 3) is a studio or 1-bed flat let as a self-contained home, charged per unit per week. Switching tabs takes you back to a fresh input form.

**Q89. How do I set the council area and number of beds, and why does it matter?**

Before you can analyse a deal you must pick a Council Area and a Number of Beds (1 to 6). The UCWS rent is the council's own per-room weekly rate for that bed band (from the Allianz rate sheet) — it is not standard LHA, which is why it is set per council. To save time you can type a Postcode and the tool auto-fills the matching council for you. The "Analyse This Deal" button stays locked until both a council and a bed count are chosen, because the whole calculation depends on them.

**Q90. What does each field in "Property Details" mean?**

Property Address is the address of the property. Purchase Price / Value (£) is what you are paying for it, or its value. Number of Bedrooms/Unit is the rooms available for UCWS tenants. Current Market Rent (£/month) is the ordinary rent you would get without UCWS, used for comparison. Mortgage Rate (%) is your interest rate — set it to 0 if there is no mortgage. Mortgage Term (years) is the mortgage length — set to 0 if no mortgage. LTV (%) is loan-to-value, the percentage of the property value you are borrowing.

**Q91. What are the "Setup Costs" and "Monthly Costs" fields?**

Setup Costs are the one-off costs to get the property ready: Onboarding Fee (£250 + VAT), Electricity Certification (EICR), Gas Certification, EPC, Furniture (entered per room — note this per-room figure is not added into the setup total), Fire Alarms and Fire Doors (HMO and Joint only), Fire Safety Certificate, Decorating / Minor Works, and Broker / Mortgage Fees. A 10% contingency is added automatically on the results screen.

Monthly Costs are the recurring costs, applied to both the standard and UCWS scenarios: Bills (gas/electric/water/council tax), Insurance, Maintenance Reserve, and Management / Other.

**Q92. How is "Gross / Month" calculated?**

Gross per month = the council's weekly rate × the number of beds × 52 ÷ 12. In words: multiply the per-room (or per-unit) weekly rate for your council by the number of beds to get the weekly rent, multiply by 52 to get the year, then divide by 12 to get the month. For example, at a council rate of £355.51 per room per week with 4 beds: 355.51 × 4 = £1,422.04 per week; × 52 = £73,946.08 per year; ÷ 12 = £6,162.17 gross per month. These figures are worked out for you from your inputs and the council rate — you do not type them in.

**Q93. What do "Your Share — 75%" and "Deduction — 25%" mean?**

The UCWS rent is split between you and UCWS. You (the owner) keep 75% and UCWS takes a 25% management fee. So Your Share per month = Gross/Month × 0.75, and the Deduction per month = Gross/Month × 0.25. It is your 75% share that is carried into the cashflow and ROI figures.

**Q94. What are the 100%, 85% and 70% figures?**

They are occupancy scenarios. The analyser shows your income and net cashflow at full occupancy (100%) and also at 85% and 70%, so you can see how the deal holds up if some rooms are empty (voids). The headline ROI and the GO / NO-GO decision are based on the 100% figure.

**Q95. What does the results screen show, and what makes a deal a "GO"?**

At the top you get a GO or NO-GO banner with the reason. Below that are four headline figures: Setup Costs (total), Payback Period, Extra Monthly (extra cashflow versus an ordinary let), and ROI @ 100%. Then there is a gross-income table (Standard / 100% / 85% / 70%, by week, month and year), the net cashflow for each occupancy level, and full breakdowns of the monthly and setup costs. The target is an ROI of at least 20% with positive monthly cashflow — that returns a GO. An ROI below 20%, or negative cashflow, returns a NO-GO with the reason shown.

**Q96. How do I move through the tool and re-run a deal?**

Pick a strategy tab, set the council and beds (or enter a postcode), fill in the three sections of fields, then click "Analyse This Deal". On the results screen you can click "Adjust Inputs" to change your numbers, "New Analysis" to start a fresh deal, or "Back" to return to the form.

**SECTION 20: USING THE DEMAND MAP**

**Q97. What is the Demand Map and where do I find it?**

The Demand Map is an interactive, colour-coded map of all 296 local authorities (council areas) in England, found in the portal's "Demand Map" tab. Its purpose is exempt-housing investment intelligence: it shows where there is the most need for supported and exempt accommodation, so you can target the right areas. It combines nine official UK government datasets into one picture and refreshes automatically every quarter. It uses only public statistics — no personal data. For any questions about the Demand Map, contact info@brandscaling.co.uk.

**Q98. How do I read the colours (the heatmap)?**

Each council is shaded by a value, and a more intense colour means higher demand (or a higher value) for whichever layer is selected. By default the map shows the Overall Housing Demand Score. You can switch to a single layer — for example House Prices, which runs from green (affordable) to red (expensive) — to colour the map by just that measure, or tick several layers at once and the map blends their colours together. Grey means "no data reported for this area"; it does not mean low demand, just that no figure is available for that council on that layer.

**Q99. What is the Overall Housing Demand Score and what do the bands mean?**

It is a single pre-calculated score from 0 to 100, where higher means more need for supported housing. Councils fall into five bands: Critical Demand (65–100), Very High Demand (50–64), High Demand (35–49), Moderate Demand (20–34) and Low Demand (0–19). The coloured ring in the detail panel shows the exact score and band for the area you click.

**Q100. What goes into the demand score?**

It is a weighted blend of eight ingredients: the benefit claimant rate (20%), deprivation as measured by the IMD (15%), prison leavers (15%), the over-65s population share (10%), the children and young-people share (10%), the LHA rent shortfall (10%), NHS staff vacancies (10%), and a carried-forward base score (10%). Higher claimant rates, more deprivation, more vulnerable residents and bigger rent gaps all push the score up.

**Q101. What do the twelve map layers show?**

Overall Housing Demand (the combined score); Investment Opportunity (where demand is high and property is cheaper, so the yield potential is better); House Prices (average price, affordable to expensive); Housing Benefit, Universal Credit and PIP Claimants (the three DWP benefits — see Q102); Over-65s Housing Need and Family Housing Need (population age groups — see Q103); Prison Leavers (estimated ex-offenders needing housing); NHS Vacancies (staff vacancy rate, a proxy for key-worker housing pressure); IMD 2025 Deprivation (the government's official deprivation ranking); and LHA Shortfall Zones (the rent gap — see Q104).

**Q102. What do the DWP benefit layers (Housing Benefit, Universal Credit, PIP) show?**

They show how many people in each council are on each benefit, from DWP Stat-Xplore. Housing Benefit is the older housing support (being replaced by Universal Credit); Universal Credit is the main modern benefit (households with a housing element); and PIP, Personal Independence Payment, is a disability benefit used here as an indicator of supported-living need. A bigger claimant base generally signals stronger demand for exempt accommodation.

**Q103. What do "Over-65s Housing Need" and "Family Housing Need" show?**

Over-65s Housing Need is the share of the council's population aged 65 and over — a proxy for demand for sheltered and older-person housing. Family Housing Need is the share aged 0 to 19 — a proxy for family housing demand. Both come from ONS population estimates and are scaled from low to high across England.

**Q104. What is "LHA Shortfall Zones"?**

LHA stands for Local Housing Allowance — the cap on how much rent housing benefit will cover. This layer shows how much the local market rent exceeds that cap, scaled from Minimal to Severe. A larger shortfall means renters on benefits struggle more to afford ordinary local rents, which strengthens the case for the exempt-accommodation model, where higher rents are payable. It is calculated per rental-market area from VOA figures.

**Q105. What does the detail panel show when I click a council?**

It opens a panel with: the demand score ring and band; Property Market (average price, growth since 2019, and an investment score — the panel notes that lower prices mean higher yield potential); an Investment Signal (a plain-English recommendation generated from that area's data); Tenant Demand Sectors (the actual head-counts of people who may need supported accommodation — Housing Benefit, Universal Credit, PIP, elderly, children, prison leavers, probation caseload, NHS vacancies and the ONS claimant count); Benefit Claimant Trends (how claims changed from 2019 to 2025); and the IMD 2025 deprivation score with the area's national rank.

**Q106. Why are some areas grey or "not reported"?**

Grey means there is no figure available for that council on the selected layer — it is missing data, not zero demand. Most layers now cover all 296 councils; a small number of genuine gaps remain at source (for example, the Isles of Scilly has no published house-price figure). When several layers are combined, an area with no data on one of them contributes a neutral dark shade to the blend.

**Q107. Where does the Demand Map data come from and how often is it updated?**

It draws on nine official government sources: DWP Stat-Xplore (Housing Benefit, Universal Credit, PIP), ONS Claimant Count, MHCLG Indices of Deprivation 2025, VOA Local Housing Allowance, HM Land Registry house prices, ONS population estimates, MoJ probation data, NHS vacancy statistics, and DfE Children in Need. The data is refreshed automatically every quarter. Some figures (prison leavers and NHS vacancies) are modelled estimates allocated to each council, so treat them as directional indicators rather than exact local counts.

**Q6. Does the UCWS model impact planning or licensing requirements for existing HMOs in certain areas such as A4 zones or London?**

The UCES model's impact on planning and licensing depends on how the property is structured and operated, not on the area type. Under the UCES exempt accommodation framework, properties are designed to operate as C3(b) — up to 6 residents living together as a single household with mandatory care and support. When a property genuinely operates this way, it may remain within the C3 planning use class and does not require an HMO licence.

However, the key point is: if a property does not operate as a single household with structured support, the local planning authority may treat it as C4 (small HMO) or Sui Generis (large HMO), in which case Article 4 directions or full planning permission may apply.

This means:
- Article 4 areas: Article 4 directions typically remove permitted development rights for C3→C4 changes. If your property genuinely operates within C3(b), it may not trigger a change of use. But whether Article 4 applies depends on how the Local Authority classifies the property's actual use — it is not automatic.
- London and other areas: The same principle applies regardless of location. The exemption from planning/licensing is based on the C3(b) structure and support model, not on the area.

The course explains the use classes, the C3(b) position, and includes an action task to check whether your target postcode sits in an Article 4 area.

For specific advice on your property's planning status in a particular area, you should check with your local planning authority or take professional planning advice. The UCES model can impact planning and licensing — especially in Article 4 zones or London. Bringing an existing licensed HMO into the system means it's no longer classed as a licensed HMO. If you later want to revert, you may not get HMO status back. Always get independent planning advice and flag Article 4 or licensing questions early.

**Q12. What are the risks associated with using the UCWS program?**

The key risks associated with the UCES programme are:

1. Void periods — You as the landlord are responsible for all void periods. There is no guaranteed rent under this model. However, the demand for supported housing significantly outstrips supply — the government estimates an additional 361,700 to 640,700 units are needed by 2040 — which helps to minimise void risk in practice.

2. Property damage — This is covered by insurance. You should arrange cover that reflects how the property is used (buildings, contents, and public liability), taking advice from a specialist FCA-authorised insurance broker.

3. Tenant issues — These are managed by the tenant support services company, which is approved by the Registered Provider and included within the 25% deductions.

4. Lender restrictions — Some lenders may need to approve the arrangement. You should check with your mortgage broker before proceeding, as lender criteria vary.

The training covers risk mitigation strategies for each of these in detail, so you will have a full understanding of how to manage them before you onboard your first property.

**Q13. What are the tenant turnover rates like for UCWS properties compared to traditional Buy-to-Let investments?**

Under the UCHMO model, tenants are on an Excluded Licence Agreement with a 7-day notice period (not an AST). There is no minimum stay period. Turnover rates vary depending on the tenant's circumstances and support needs. However, demand for supported housing significantly outstrips supply (government estimates 361,700–640,700 additional units needed by 2040), so re-letting is typically faster than standard BTL. Void risk remains your responsibility, but strong demand helps minimise void periods in practice.

**SECTION 90: UCWS-TO-UCES TRANSITION**

**Q1. Is the UCWS strategy exempt from planning permission in an Article 4 area?**

Under the UCHMO model, properties are designed to operate as C3(b) — up to 6 residents living together as a single household with mandatory support. When a property genuinely operates this way, it may remain within the C3 planning use class and not trigger a C3 to C4 change of use. Article 4 directions typically remove permitted development rights for C3→C4 changes. If your property genuinely operates within C3(b), it may not be affected by Article 4. However, whether Article 4 applies depends on how the Local Authority classifies the property's actual use — it is not automatic. The course explains the use classes, the C3(b) position, and includes an action task to check whether your target postcode sits in an Article 4 area. Always get independent planning advice and flag Article 4 questions early.
